# SuperDARN

The Super Dual Auroral Radar Network — a global array of HF (8–20 MHz) coherent-scatter radars covering the high-latitude ionosphere in both hemispheres. Each radar transmits HF pulses that refract in the ionosphere and scatter back to the ground via Bragg backscatter from decametre-scale field-aligned plasma irregularities. Multi-beam Doppler measurements across the network are combined into maps of the ionospheric electrostatic potential $\Phi$, whose contours are streamlines of the $\mathbf{E}\times\mathbf{B}$ convection flow.

## Role in polar cap research

**Convection mapping.** SuperDARN provides the primary large-scale convection maps used in polar cap science. Fitted potential maps overlaid on GPS TEC images or airglow data allow direct visualization of how the two-cell convection pattern draws plasma from the dayside into the polar cap, which structures co-move with the $\mathbf{E}\times\mathbf{B}$ flow ([[Polar Cap Patch|patches]]), and which propagate at different velocities (TIDs). The convection patterns sorted by IMF clock angle (Thomas & Shepherd 2018) show the expected two-cell pattern for southward $B_z$ and a four-cell pattern for northward $B_z$.

**HF backscatter from patches.** [[Polar Cap Patch|Polar cap patches]] are filled with small-scale irregularities ($< 1$ km) generated by the [[Ionospheric Instabilities|gradient-drift instability]]. These irregularities are field-aligned (perpendicular to $\mathbf{B}$). Patches are dense enough to refract HF rays to near-perpendicularity with the geomagnetic field, satisfying the Bragg condition, so patches appear as collectively-drifting regions of enhanced backscatter power in range-time-intensity (RTI) plots. A one-to-one spatial correspondence between Rankin Inlet SuperDARN HF backscatter and 630 nm airglow patches at Resolute Bay has been demonstrated observationally.

## Data products

- **Line-of-sight Doppler velocity** from each backscatter cell — primary measurement.
- **Fitted convection maps** via spherical harmonic expansion (Ruohoniemi & Baker model); gaps in backscatter coverage are filled by an empirical statistical model.
- **Backscatter power and spectral width** — used to distinguish ionospheric scatter (narrow spectral width) from ground/sea scatter (zero Doppler) and to infer irregularity intensity.

## Key limitation

SuperDARN requires pre-existing plasma irregularities to produce backscatter — it only "sees" regions where the plasma is already irregular. During geomagnetically quiet periods or in smooth-density plasma, coverage is sparse and convection maps are poorly constrained. SuperDARN cannot directly measure electron density.

## HF Ray Tracing

Patch-enhanced backscatter and ground-scatter skip zones depend on the ionospheric ray-path geometry. The Haselgrove-equation ray tracing procedure of [[Coleman 1992 Ionospheric Ray Tracing]] (implemented as the HASEL FORTRAN subroutine) provides a general-purpose method for tracing HF rays through realistic 3-D ionospheres, computing phase path, group path, and Doppler shift while accounting for the full Appleton-Hartree refractive index and Earth's dipole magnetic field. See also [[HF Radio Propagation]].

## Velocity calibration against ISR

[[Gilles 2018 RISR SuperDARN Velocity Comparison]] compared SuperDARN line-of-sight velocities from Rankin Inlet and Clyde River against [[RISR-N]] over 40 days ($5.2\times10^5$ coincident measurements). SuperDARN F-region velocities are systematically ~75–85% of RISR-N before applying a refractive-index correction; after correction the two systems agree. Daytime SuperDARN data are significantly contaminated by E-region echoes and ground/sea scatter that bias low-velocity measurements toward zero — proper screening is necessary before deriving convection statistics.

## Poynting flux and echo geolocation

[[Perry 2021 SuperDARN Poynting Flux]] models the spatial Poynting flux profile of the Saskatoon SuperDARN radar by ray-tracing through the IRI ionosphere and validates against CASSIOPE/e-POP RRI antenna voltages during 5 conjunctions (August 2017). The great-circle path assumption is validated for these events. E-region and F-region echoes produce distinct Poynting flux profiles, providing a tool to discriminate between the two scatter sources and to quantify geolocation uncertainty arising from refraction and multi-hop propagation.
